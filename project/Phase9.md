# Phase 9: Comprehensive Results Analysis and Interpretation
## Imbalanced Skin Lesion Classification Using Classical and GAN-Based Augmentation

## 1. Objective of Phase 9

Phase 9 focused on analyzing the results of the completed classifier experiments, comparing the effects of classical data augmentation and GAN-generated synthetic images, examining minority-class performance, and interpreting the results in relation to the project's research questions.

The objective was not to train new models. Instead, Phase 9 reused the existing experimental outputs from four classifier experiments:

- **E0:** Baseline classifier without the experimental augmentation strategies.
- **E2:** Classical augmentation for minority-class top-up.
- **E3:** DCGAN-Aug synthetic-image augmentation.
- **E4:** WGAN-GP synthetic-image augmentation.

The analysis examined four primary classification metrics—macro-F1, accuracy, balanced accuracy, and AUC—alongside per-class recall, confusion matrices, training curves, and the existing GAN Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) evaluations.

The optional Phase 8 extensions were skipped. Consequently, Phase 9 does not evaluate additional synthetic-data quantities, combined GAN and classical augmentation, or the other proposed optional experiments.

## 2. Experimental Setup and Evaluation Protocol

### 2.1 Dataset

The project used the HAM10000 skin-lesion image dataset, containing 10,015 dermatoscopic images across seven diagnostic classes.

The class identifiers used in the project were:

| Class ID | Class abbreviation | Diagnostic category |
|---:|---|---|
| 0 | AKIEC | Actinic keratoses and intraepithelial carcinoma |
| 1 | BCC | Basal cell carcinoma |
| 2 | BKL | Benign keratosis-like lesions |
| 3 | DF | Dermatofibroma |
| 4 | MEL | Melanoma |
| 5 | NV | Melanocytic nevi |
| 6 | VASC | Vascular lesions |

The GAN image cache used 64 × 64 RGB images. The classifier used a ResNet18-based model with images resized to 224 × 224 for model input.

The original dataset was imbalanced. In the training split, NV contained 4,694 images, whereas DF contained only 80 and VASC contained 100. AKIEC contained 228 images.

This imbalance motivated the investigation of augmentation methods.

### 2.2 Fixed dataset split

The experiments used the existing fixed split stored in `cache/splits.csv`.

| Split | Number of images |
|---|---:|
| Training | 7,009 |
| Validation | 1,503 |
| Test | 1,503 |
| Total | 10,015 |

The split was kept fixed across experiments to maintain comparability.

Validation data was used for model selection and early stopping. Final metrics were computed on the test set, which contained real images rather than GAN-generated images.

Keeping the test set real-only is important: it evaluates how the trained classifier generalizes to held-out real images instead of measuring performance on synthetic examples.

### 2.3 Classifier configuration

The core classifier experiments used the same general ResNet18-based classification setup:

- Pretrained ResNet18 backbone.
- Cross-entropy loss.
- Adam optimizer with a learning rate of \(10^{-4}\).
- Early stopping based on validation macro-F1, with patience of five epochs.
- Three random seeds: 0, 1, and 2.
- Maximum of 20 epochs.

E0 provided the baseline. E2 applied classical augmentation to top-up examples from minority classes. E3 and E4 used synthetic images generated by the two active GAN candidates.

### 2.4 Experiment definitions

**E0 — Baseline**

The baseline classifier used the original training images without the experimental minority-class top-up strategy.

**E2 — Classical augmentation**

Classical transformations were used to generate additional training examples for AKIEC, DF, and VASC. The configured transformations included horizontal and vertical flips, rotation up to 30 degrees, and color jitter.

The top-up target was 359 images per selected class, matching the original BCC training count. This required 131 additional AKIEC examples, 279 DF examples, and 259 VASC examples, giving 669 additional training examples in total.

**E3 — DCGAN-Aug**

This experiment used synthetic images from the active DCGAN-Aug candidate at epoch 200.

**E4 — WGAN-GP**

This experiment used synthetic images from the active WGAN-GP candidate at epoch 200.

For both GAN experiments, the final synthetic training set contained 669 images: 131 AKIEC, 279 DF, and 259 VASC images. No synthetic examples were added to BCC, BKL, MEL, or NV.

The quality filter based on the E0 classifier was disabled. Therefore, the synthetic samples were not filtered by classifier confidence.

### 2.5 Reproducibility and evaluation consistency

All four experiments used three seeds and the same fixed data split. Each experiment's seed-specific directory contained the saved best checkpoint, configuration, run metadata, test metrics, confusion matrix, and training log.

This allowed Phase 9 to compare the experiments without retraining the classifiers.

The three seeds provide a limited estimate of variability caused by the training procedure. They do not constitute three independent datasets, because the same fixed test set was used for every seed.

### 3.2 Confusion-matrix analysis

For every experiment, the script loaded the three seed-specific test confusion matrices.

The matrix convention was verified from the original CSV format:

- Rows represent the true class.
- Columns represent the predicted class.
- Diagonal entries represent correct predictions.
- Off-diagonal entries represent misclassifications.

For each experiment, the script generated:

- Summed confusion-matrix counts across the available seeds.
- A row-normalized confusion matrix.
- A heatmap of the normalized matrix.

The row-normalized matrix is calculated as:

\[
C^{\mathrm{norm}}_{ij}
=
\frac{C_{ij}}{\sum_j C_{ij}}
\]

where \(C_{ij}\) is the count for true class \(i\) predicted as class \(j\).

This normalization allows the relative prediction patterns of different true classes to be compared even when their frequencies differ.

Generated artifacts include:

- `tables/E0_confusion_counts_sum.csv`
- `tables/E2_confusion_counts_sum.csv`
- `tables/E3_confusion_counts_sum.csv`
- `tables/E4_confusion_counts_sum.csv`
- Corresponding `*_confusion_row_normalized.csv` files.
- Corresponding confusion-matrix PNG plots under `plots/`.

**Interpretation caution:** the summed matrices represent repeated predictions on the same test set across three seeds. Their entries should not be interpreted as observations from three times as many independent patients.

### 3.3 Per-class recall analysis

The script calculated class recall from each seed's confusion matrix:

\[
\operatorname{Recall}_c
=
\frac{TP_c}{TP_c+FN_c}
\]

It then calculated mean recall and sample standard deviation for each class and experiment.

Generated artifacts:

- `tables/per_seed_class_recall.csv`
- `tables/per_class_recall_mean_sd.csv`
- `tables/per_class_recall_wide.csv`
- `plots/per_class_recall.png`

This analysis is important because overall accuracy can be dominated by NV, the most frequent class. Per-class recall reveals whether a model correctly identifies less common lesion categories.

### 3.4 MEL-versus-NV and minority-class analysis

### 3.5 Training and validation curves

## 4. Headline Classification Results

All results below are mean ± sample standard deviation across three seeds. The standard deviations describe variation across these runs; they are not confidence intervals.

| Experiment | Macro-F1 | Accuracy | Balanced accuracy | AUC |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| E0 — Baseline | 0.6345 ± 0.0215 | 0.8004 ± 0.0074 | 0.6068 ± 0.0414 | 0.9343 ± 0.0066 |
| E2 — Classical augmentation | **0.6559 ± 0.0154** | **0.8035 ± 0.0097** | 0.6291 ± 0.0187 | 0.9352 ± 0.0123 |
| E3 — DCGAN-Aug | 0.6124 ± 0.0130 | 0.7949 ± 0.0037 | 0.5979 ± 0.0123 | **0.9361 ± 0.0026** |
| E4 — WGAN-GP | 0.6515 ± 0.0095 | 0.8028 ± 0.0017 | **0.6331 ± 0.0025** | 0.9347 ± 0.0070 |

### 4.1 Macro-F1

Macro-F1 calculates the F1-score independently for each class and then takes the unweighted mean:

\[
F1_{\mathrm{macro}}
=
\frac{1}{7}\sum_{c=1}^{7}F1_c
\]

Each class therefore contributes equally to the metric, regardless of its number of images.

E2 obtained the highest observed mean macro-F1, 0.6559, compared with 0.6345 for E0. The absolute difference was approximately 0.0215, or 2.15 percentage points on the macro-F1 scale.

E4 achieved 0.6515, an absolute improvement of approximately 0.0170 over E0.

E3 achieved 0.6124, approximately 0.0221 below E0.

The results therefore show that classical augmentation produced the strongest observed macro-F1, WGAN-GP performed relatively close to the baseline, and DCGAN-Aug did not improve the aggregate macro-F1.

The three-seed results do not establish that the observed differences are statistically significant.

### 4.2 Accuracy

E2 achieved the highest mean accuracy at 0.8035, compared with 0.8004 for E0. E4 achieved 0.8028, while E3 achieved 0.7949.

The accuracy differences are small. Accuracy alone would not adequately describe the impact of augmentation in this imbalanced dataset.

A classifier can achieve high accuracy by correctly predicting the dominant NV class while still misclassifying a substantial fraction of less frequent classes.

### 4.3 Balanced accuracy

Balanced accuracy averages recall across classes:

\[
BA
=
\frac{1}{7}\sum_{c=1}^{7}\operatorname{Recall}_c
\]

E4 achieved the highest mean balanced accuracy, 0.6331, followed by E2 at 0.6291. E0 achieved 0.6068, and E3 achieved 0.5979.

The E4 improvement over E0 was approximately 0.0263. This suggests that WGAN-GP augmentation may have helped distribute recognition performance more evenly across classes in these runs.

However, the per-class results reveal that the gains were not uniform: E4 improved some classes while reducing MEL recall.

### 4.4 AUC

E3 achieved the highest mean AUC at 0.9361, followed by E2 at 0.9352, E4 at 0.9347, and E0 at 0.9343.

These differences are small.

AUC summarizes discrimination across classification thresholds, whereas the reported macro-F1, accuracy, and recall are based on the selected classification decisions. A model can therefore have a slightly higher AUC without having the highest macro-F1 or balanced accuracy.

The E3 result illustrates this distinction: it had the highest observed mean AUC but the lowest mean macro-F1 and balanced accuracy.

AUC should not be interpreted as evidence that E3 is the best overall classifier.

### 4.5 Overall interpretation

No experiment led on every metric.

- E2 led on macro-F1 and accuracy.
- E4 led on balanced accuracy.
- E3 led on mean AUC.
- E3 had the lowest mean macro-F1 and balanced accuracy.

The aggregate metrics support a nuanced conclusion rather than a claim that one augmentation method is universally superior.

## 5. Per-Class Recall Analysis

The mean recalls across seeds were:

| AKIEC | 0.4898 | **0.4966** | 0.4762 | 0.4898 |
| BCC | 0.6838 | 0.6282 | 0.6838 | **0.7094** |
| BKL | 0.5455 | 0.5737 | **0.5818** | 0.5657 |
| DF | 0.2549 | 0.3725 | 0.2353 | **0.3922** |

| NV | 0.9264 | 0.9280 | 0.9207 | **0.9297** |
| VASC | 0.8571 | **0.9206** | 0.8413 | 0.9048 |

### 5.1 AKIEC

E2 achieved the highest mean AKIEC recall, 0.4966, compared with 0.4898 for E0.

E4 matched the baseline mean, while E3 was lower at 0.4762.

The E2 improvement was small. These results do not show a consistent AKIEC recall improvement from both GAN approaches.

### 5.2 BCC

E4 achieved the highest BCC recall at 0.7094, compared with 0.6838 for E0.

E3 matched the baseline, whereas E2 fell to 0.6282.

The E2 result is an important reminder that top-up augmentation of selected minority classes does not necessarily improve every other class. Training changes can affect decision boundaries and trade-offs between categories.

### 5.3 BKL

BKL recall increased from 0.5455 for E0 to 0.5737 for E2, 0.5818 for E3, and 0.5657 for E4.

E3 had the highest BKL recall, even though its aggregate macro-F1 was the lowest.

This demonstrates that a method can improve one class without improving overall classification performance.

### 5.4 DF

DF showed one of the clearest improvements for the augmentation experiments.

- E0: 0.2549
- E2: 0.3725
- E3: 0.2353
- E4: 0.3922

E2 improved mean DF recall by approximately 11.76 percentage points relative to E0. E4 improved it by approximately 13.73 percentage points.

E3 decreased DF recall by approximately 1.96 percentage points.

The WGAN-GP and classical augmentation results suggest that additional training examples may have helped the classifier identify DF more frequently. However, DF is a very small class, and the results should be interpreted with the small number of real examples in mind.

Higher recall does not guarantee higher precision. It is possible for a classifier to identify more actual DF examples while also incorrectly assigning more non-DF lesions to DF.

### 5.5 MEL

MEL recall was:

- E0: 0.4901
- E2: 0.4841
- E3: 0.4464
- E4: 0.4405

E0 had the highest mean MEL recall.

Relative to E0, MEL recall decreased by approximately 0.60 percentage points for E2, 4.37 percentage points for E3, and 4.96 percentage points for E4.

This is a critical trade-off in the results. The methods with better minority-class recall in several categories did not improve MEL recall.

Because melanoma is clinically important, the decline should be reported explicitly rather than hidden by improvements in macro-F1 or balanced accuracy.

These experiments are research evaluations, not evidence of diagnostic safety. Clinical conclusions would require additional validation, calibration, precision and false-negative analysis, and evaluation in appropriate clinical settings.

### 5.6 NV

NV recall was high across all four experiments:

- E0: 0.9264
- E2: 0.9280
- E3: 0.9207
- E4: 0.9297

E4 had the highest mean NV recall, although the differences were small.

Because NV is the dominant class, its high recall contributes substantially to the observed overall accuracy. The consistently high NV recall should not obscure the more difficult performance on classes such as DF, AKIEC, and MEL.

### 5.7 VASC

VASC recall increased from 0.8571 for E0 to 0.9206 for E2 and 0.9048 for E4. E3 achieved 0.8413.

E2 showed the highest VASC recall, with an improvement of approximately 6.35 percentage points relative to the baseline. E4 improved by approximately 4.76 percentage points, while E3 declined by approximately 1.59 percentage points.

As with DF, these improvements are encouraging observations but do not by themselves establish better precision or reliable clinical classification.

### 5.8 Summary of class-level trade-offs

The strongest observed recall for each class came from different experiments:

- AKIEC: E2
- BCC: E4
- BKL: E3
- DF: E4
- MEL: E0
- NV: E4
- VASC: E2

There is no single method that achieved the highest recall in all seven classes.

E2 and E4 showed improvements for multiple minority classes, particularly DF and VASC, but MEL recall declined. E3 improved BKL recall but performed worse than the baseline for several other classes.

This class-level evidence is essential to understanding why the aggregate metrics alone are insufficient.

## 6. Confusion-Matrix Analysis

### 6.1 Why confusion matrices were analyzed

A confusion matrix identifies which classes are confused with one another. This is especially relevant for dermatoscopic images, where visually similar lesion categories can be difficult to distinguish.

The Phase 9 script generated both summed-count and row-normalized confusion matrices for each experiment.

The normalized plots support comparison of the distribution of predictions for each true class.

### 6.2 MEL-to-NV confusion

The fraction of actual MEL examples predicted as NV, calculated from the aggregate confusion matrices, was:

| Experiment | MEL predicted as NV |
|---|---:|
| E0 | 36.11% |
| E2 | 35.91% |
| E3 | 35.71% |
| E4 | 38.69% |

E3 had the lowest MEL-to-NV confusion fraction, closely followed by E2 and E0. E4 had the highest.

However, E3's total MEL recall was lower than E0. Reducing confusion with one particular class does not necessarily improve total recall: some cases may be misclassified into other categories instead.

E4's result is more concerning because it combines the highest MEL-to-NV confusion fraction with the lowest mean MEL recall of the four experiments.

### 6.3 NV-to-MEL confusion

The script also calculated the fraction of actual NV examples predicted as MEL. This is important because confusion between MEL and NV is not necessarily symmetric.

The generated `mel_nv_and_minority_analysis.csv` contains this quantity, but the exact values are not reproduced here because they have not been independently inspected in the conversation.

### 6.4 Minority-class errors

The class-recall results indicate that DF and VASC benefited from E2 and E4 relative to the baseline, while E3 did not produce the same improvement.

The normalized confusion matrices should be used alongside the recall table to identify where the remaining errors occur. They should not be interpreted as evidence that all errors have equal clinical consequences.

### 6.5 Limitation of aggregate confusion matrices

Summing the three seed-specific matrices provides a convenient overall visualization, but it combines repeated predictions for the same held-out images.

Consequently, the aggregate matrix is not a larger independent test cohort. The seed-level variation remains relevant, and more formal uncertainty estimation would be needed for stronger inferential claims.

## 7. GAN Image Quality and FID Analysis

### 7.1 Purpose of FID

FID, or Fréchet Inception Distance, compares feature distributions between real and generated images using an Inception-based feature representation.

Lower FID generally indicates closer agreement under this metric. It does not directly measure diagnostic usefulness, clinical realism, or the downstream value of synthetic data.

FID results were obtained from the existing GAN evaluation artifacts rather than calculated by the Phase 9 classifier-analysis script.

### 7.2 Verified E200 FID results

The E200 values were verified from `results/fid_evaluation_active/fid_results_long.csv`.

| GAN candidate | Real count | Synthetic count | Overall FID |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| DCGAN-Aug E200 | 7,009 | 7,000 | **148.2569** |
| WGAN-GP E200 | 7,009 | 7,000 | 163.3430 |

DCGAN-Aug had an FID approximately 15.09 points lower than WGAN-GP, or approximately 9.2% lower relative to WGAN-GP's value.

This establishes that DCGAN-Aug had the lower measured overall FID among these two candidates under the evaluation protocol used in the project.

It does not establish that DCGAN-Aug generated medically superior images.

### 7.3 FID versus downstream classification

The classifier results were:

| Measure | DCGAN-Aug (E3) | WGAN-GP (E4) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Overall FID | **148.2569** | 163.3430 |
| Mean macro-F1 | 0.6124 | **0.6515** |
| Mean balanced accuracy | 0.5979 | **0.6331** |
| Mean AUC | **0.9361** | 0.9347 |

DCGAN-Aug had the lower FID, but WGAN-GP achieved the higher mean macro-F1 and balanced accuracy.

This is one of the central findings of the project: **the candidate with the lower FID did not achieve the better downstream classification performance.**

The result demonstrates that FID alone was not a sufficient criterion for selecting the GAN augmentation strategy in these experiments.

The AUC results also show that the comparison depends on the metric. DCGAN-Aug had a slightly higher mean AUC, but that did not translate into a higher macro-F1 or balanced accuracy.

### 7.4 Limits of the FID comparison

The FID values should be interpreted cautiously for several reasons:

1. FID measures feature-distribution similarity, not lesion diagnostic correctness.
2. Inception features are not a dedicated medical-image realism metric.
3. FID values for individual classes can be noisy, particularly for rare classes.
4. A GAN can generate images that look plausible without preserving clinically important details.
5. Downstream performance also depends on class coverage, the training procedure, the number of examples, and how generated examples affect the classifier's decision boundaries.

The FID comparison therefore complements, rather than replaces, the classifier evaluation.

## 9. Applying the Project's Decision Heuristic

The project specified a heuristic: consider a difference measurable only if the absolute mean macro-F1 gap exceeds the baseline seed standard deviation.

For E0, the baseline macro-F1 standard deviation was approximately 0.0215.

The observed mean macro-F1 gaps were:

| Comparison | Absolute macro-F1 gap | Baseline SD | Passes project heuristic? |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| E2 versus E0 | 0.0215 | 0.0215 | No, using unrounded values |
| E3 versus E0 | 0.0221 lower | 0.0215 | The gap is slightly larger, but it is a decline, not a gain |
| E4 versus E0 | 0.0170 | 0.0215 | No |

E2's gain is slightly below the unrounded baseline standard deviation, even though both round to approximately 0.0215.

E4's gain is smaller than the baseline standard deviation. E3's mean macro-F1 is lower than the baseline.

The correct project-level conclusion is that **no GAN-based experiment established a measurable macro-F1 improvement under the specified heuristic**.

This heuristic is not a formal statistical significance test. In particular, comparing an observed mean difference with a single standard deviation does not provide a calibrated p-value or confidence interval. The conclusion should be presented as the outcome of the project's predefined decision rule, not as proof that the methods are statistically equivalent or that augmentation cannot help.

## 10. Answers to the Research Questions

### RQ1: Does GAN-based synthetic-image augmentation improve skin-lesion classification performance under class imbalance?

**Answer:** A consistent aggregate improvement was not established in the completed experiments.

E3, using DCGAN-Aug images, achieved a lower mean macro-F1 and balanced accuracy than E0. E4, using WGAN-GP images, improved both metrics relative to E0, but its macro-F1 improvement did not pass the project's predefined heuristic.

E2 achieved the highest mean macro-F1, indicating that classical augmentation was the strongest observed approach by that metric in the completed experiment set.

The result is specific to the implemented dataset, synthetic sample count, GAN candidates, classifier, and training procedure. It does not establish that GAN augmentation is generally ineffective.

### RQ2: Does a better FID correspond to better downstream classification performance?

**Answer:** Not in this experiment.

DCGAN-Aug E200 had the lower FID, 148.2569, compared with 163.3430 for WGAN-GP E200. However, the WGAN-GP classifier achieved a higher mean macro-F1 and balanced accuracy.

The result shows that a lower FID did not guarantee a better downstream classifier in this study. FID should therefore be combined with downstream classification evaluation and appropriate image-quality checks rather than used as the sole selection criterion.

### RQ3: Does increasing the quantity of synthetic training data improve performance?

**Answer:** Not evaluated in Phase 9.

The optional quantity extension was skipped. The completed E3 and E4 experiments used the same synthetic top-up quantity of 669 examples.

Consequently, the available results cannot establish whether a larger or smaller synthetic dataset would improve performance.

### RQ4: Does combining GAN augmentation with classical augmentation improve performance?

**Answer:** Not evaluated in Phase 9.

The optional combined-augmentation extension was skipped. The completed results do not isolate the effect of combining the two methods.

No positive or negative conclusion about the combined strategy should be claimed.

## 11. Limitations

### 11.1 Limited number of seeds

Each experiment used three seeds. This provides a basic measure of run-to-run variability but is not sufficient for strong statistical claims.

The standard deviation across seeds is not a confidence interval, and the fixed test set means the runs are not independent test cohorts.

### 11.2 Single dataset

The experiments used HAM10000. Results may differ on other datasets, imaging devices, populations, acquisition conditions, or diagnostic distributions.

External validation is needed before generalizing beyond the current dataset.

### 11.3 Low-resolution GAN images

GAN training and synthetic-data preparation used 64 × 64 RGB images. Such resolution may not preserve subtle lesion structures and details that matter for medical-image interpretation.

Upsampling the generated images to 224 × 224 for classifier input does not recover information that was absent at the original resolution.

### 11.4 FID is not a medical realism metric

The FID comparison is useful for relative evaluation under the chosen feature representation, but it does not demonstrate clinical plausibility or diagnostic correctness.

Per-class FID values are also less stable when class sample counts are small.

### 11.5 No classifier-based synthetic quality filter

The quality filter was disabled. The analysis therefore does not determine whether filtering generated samples by a carefully validated criterion would change the downstream results.

A classifier-confidence filter would itself require careful evaluation to avoid circular selection bias.

### 11.6 Recall does not capture every type of error

Recall measures the fraction of actual class members correctly identified. It does not measure the proportion of predictions for a class that are correct.

Precision, class-specific F1, false-negative counts, calibration, and confusion patterns are also important. This is particularly relevant to MEL, where recall declined in E2, E3, and E4 relative to E0.

### 11.7 Optional extensions were not run

The quantity experiment, combined augmentation, and other Phase 8 extensions were skipped. The research questions that depend on those experiments remain unanswered.

### 11.8 No formal inferential comparison

The project applied its predefined macro-F1 heuristic, but did not establish a paired bootstrap confidence interval or another formal inferential comparison.

The observed metric differences should therefore be described as experimental findings rather than statistically significant improvements.

### 11.9 Not a clinical diagnostic system

This project is a research and educational prototype. The results do not establish clinical readiness, patient benefit, or diagnostic safety.

## 12. Overall Discussion

The central finding is that the effect of augmentation depended on the method and the evaluation metric.

Classical augmentation achieved the highest observed mean macro-F1 and accuracy. WGAN-GP augmentation achieved the highest balanced accuracy and showed substantial improvements in DF and VASC recall relative to the baseline. However, WGAN-GP also showed lower MEL recall and greater MEL-to-NV confusion.

DCGAN-Aug achieved the best FID and the highest mean AUC among the four experiments, but it did not produce the best macro-F1 or balanced accuracy. It also showed lower DF, MEL, NV, and VASC recall than the baseline.

These findings illustrate three important principles.

First, class imbalance cannot be evaluated solely through accuracy. Per-class recall and balanced metrics are necessary to understand how an augmentation strategy affects less frequent categories.

Second, improved performance for selected minority classes can coexist with deteriorating performance for another important class. Augmentation should therefore be assessed for its full class-level effects rather than judged only by the classes that improve.

Third, GAN image-distribution similarity and downstream classification performance are distinct objectives. The lower FID of DCGAN-Aug did not translate into better aggregate classifier performance compared with WGAN-GP.

Taken together, the results do not establish that GAN augmentation is superior to classical augmentation in this setup. They instead suggest that the utility of synthetic images depends on the GAN method, the selected synthetic-data quantity, the classifier's training behavior, and the metric used to assess performance.
